Which camera to buy to start photographing in 2026

GearBy Matías SilvaNovember 13, 20265 min read

The best camera to start with is not the most expensive one or the one with the most megapixels. It is the one you will actually carry and that lets you learn to control exposure. This guide sorts the options by budget, with reference prices from Argentina, so you can choose without overspending.

Before looking at models: four questions

  1. What do you want to photograph? Portraits and street ask for little. Sport, surf or wildlife need good autofocus and, later on, a telephoto lens.
  2. What is your total budget? Count body and lens together. An expensive body with a poor lens delivers less than the other way round.
  3. How much weight will you carry? A camera left in a drawer takes no photographs.
  4. Do you have a recent phone? If so, you already have a camera to start with.

What matters and what does not

ConceptWhat it isHow much it should weigh in
Mirrorless or DSLRMirrorless cameras show the photo in the viewfinder as it will come out. DSLRs show the scene through a mirror.Today mirrorless is the better choice: brands have almost stopped releasing new DSLRs. A used DSLR is still a cheap way to learn.
Sensor sizeAPS-C is the format of entry-level cameras. Full frame is larger.Full frame performs better in low light and blurs the background more, but the gear costs and weighs more. To learn, APS-C is enough.
MountEach brand's lens system.It matters more than the body. You will change cameras, but the lenses stay.
ViewfinderThe small window you look through.Very useful in bright sun, when the screen is hard to see.
MegapixelsThe sensor's resolution.Not much. Between 20 and 26 MP is plenty for social media, the web and large prints.

If you want the cheapest new option, the Canon EOS R100 is usually the lowest-priced mirrorless camera with a viewfinder. It is slower and has fewer features, but it does the job for learning.

Why I chose the Sony A7 III

I learned on a Canon Rebel T5i, an entry-level DSLR. It taught me exposure, but in Nazaré it fell short in low light and with moving water. When I decided to take the step, I chose the Sony A7 III for three reasons:

  • Full frame with good low-light performance, for sunsets, nights and grey winter days.
  • The E mount, with lenses from Sony and also from Sigma and Tamron, at every price point.
  • Price: it came out in 2018 and today costs far less than a new model, with features that still hold up.

You do not need that much to start. An entry-level mirrorless camera with a good lens lets you learn everything that matters.

If you buy used

  • Shutter count: ask for it. Each model has an estimated shutter life, and you want to be well below it.
  • Sensor and lens: take a photo at f/16 against the sky. Dark spots are dust on the sensor; fungus in a lens looks like cobwebs when you hold it up to the light.
  • Focus and controls: check that it focuses quickly and that every dial and button responds.
  • Paperwork: an invoice or warranty, if there is one. Be wary of prices far below the market.

The lens matters more than it seems

The kit lens is fine to start. But the biggest change in your photos often comes with a 50 mm f/1.8: it is cheap, light, very sharp and blurs the background like no kit zoom can. It was the first lens I bought for the Sony.

Three common mistakes

  1. Spending everything on the body and having nothing left for a good lens.
  2. Choosing by megapixels or 8K video when what you want is to take photographs.
  3. Buying accessories before using the camera. Go out and shoot first; then you will know what you are missing.

In short

If you are just starting, learn with what you have and, when you want to take the step, choose an entry-level mirrorless camera with a good lens. Full frame is for when you already know photography will be part of your life. Whatever the camera, what makes the difference is understanding the exposure triangle and going out to use it often.
